WHY THIS MATTERS

A hypothesis worded vaguely enough can absorb almost any evidence as "not
inconsistent" with it, which makes it look artificially strong later, not
because it's more likely true, but because it's harder to disconfirm than
its siblings. Separately, if one hypothesis specifies something (a time
period, a population, a scale) that a sibling hypothesis leaves
unspecified, evidence about that dimension will have somewhere to attach
for one hypothesis and nowhere to attach for the other — an asymmetry in
wording, not in what's actually true about the world.

WHAT TO DO

STEP 1 — PROFILE EACH HYPOTHESIS

For each hypothesis, state:
- falsifiability: low, moderate, high, or no_falsifiers_stated. Use the
  last when the hypothesis as written names nothing that would count
  against it -- a drafting gap, which is different from weak falsifiers.
  Ask specifically: what would
  count as evidence AGAINST this hypothesis? If it's hard to imagine
  anything that would count against it, that's low falsifiability,
  regardless of how plausible the hypothesis sounds.
- falsifiability_rationale: why you rated it that way.
- scope_qualifiers: every dimension this hypothesis explicitly commits to
  — timing, population, magnitude, geography, or anything else relevant
  to this domain. For each, state the dimension and what this hypothesis
  says about it.

STEP 2 — COMPARE ACROSS THE SET

For every scope dimension that ANY hypothesis specifies, check the
others: do they specify it too, leave it open for a clear and defensible
reason, or leave it open in a way that looks like an oversight? Do not
assume an omission is a mistake — some hypotheses genuinely should be
broader than others. Flag it only when the asymmetry could plausibly
change how evidence gets rated later, not just because the wording
differs.

STEP 3 — CHECK FALSIFIABILITY BALANCE

Is any hypothesis in this set notably harder to disconfirm than the
others? Do not let a hypothesis's plausibility or familiarity substitute
for checking this directly — a common, intuitive-sounding explanation
deserves exactly the same scrutiny as an unusual one.

introduce a technical term and then explain it in plain words right after
